Why Small Pull Requests Ship Faster

A note on review latency, context switching, and why the smallest diff is usually the cheapest one to land.

Process Sep 26, 2026 5 min read

Every team has that one PR that has been open for nine days. It touches 40 files, renames half the API, and mixes a refactor with three bug fixes. Nobody wants to review it, so it sits there while everyone works around it.

The math of review latency

Review cost does not scale linearly with diff size — it scales with context switches. A 50-line PR can be reviewed in the gap between two tasks. A 2,000-line PR needs an appointment. Appointments get rescheduled.

Small PRs don't just merge faster. They get better reviews, because the reviewer still has the problem in their head.

Practical rules that work

  • One PR, one reason to exist. If you can't summarize it in a single sentence, split it.
  • Refactor first, feature second — in separate PRs, never both.
  • Keep the diff under ~300 lines when possible. Mechanical changes excepted.

The fastest teams we measured kept a hard rule: no PR open for more than 24 hours without a comment. It sounds bureaucratic. It works.

What this looked like in numbers

On one team, moving from an average diff of 900 lines to 200 lines cut median time-to-merge from 31 hours to 6. Nothing else changed — not the review policy, not the people, not the tooling.

before:  avg 912 lines  →  median merge 31h
after:   avg 214 lines  →  median merge  6h

The lesson isn't "write less code." It's "ship smaller units of it."

Debugging at 2 A.M. Is a Skill, Not a Personality Trait

Notes from too many late-night sessions: how to get out of the loop instead of deeper into it.

Process Sep 20, 2026 4 min read

There's a specific kind of tunnel vision that happens when a bug refuses to reproduce. You add logs, remove logs, restart, stare at the same stack trace for the tenth time. The bug wins by making you tired.

Three habits that reliably break the loop:

  • Write down what you know. The list is always shorter than it feels. Half the "mysteries" are assumptions.
  • Bisect the system, not the code. Find the first layer where the wrong behavior appears, then stop looking above it.
  • Change one thing at a time — and write down what you changed.

The goal of debugging isn't to be smart. It's to be systematic while you're too tired to be smart.
